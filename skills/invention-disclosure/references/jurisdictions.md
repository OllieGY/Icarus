### Grace period

12 months from the inventor's own disclosure, or a disclosure derived from the inventor
(post-AIA, applications filed after 2013-03-16). This is the exception, not the norm — most
of the world has nothing like it.
Source: grace-period comparison table, Pinepat — https://www.pinepat.com/en/insights/materials/grace-period-for-patent-law-by-country-region

### Software and AI eligibility (35 U.S.C. §101)

| Date | Development | What it means for a claim |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 | *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank* | The two-step abstract-idea framework that still governs. |
| 2025-04 | *Recentive Analytics v. Fox*, 134 F.4th 1205 (Fed. Cir.) | Applying established machine learning to a new data environment, with no recited improvement to the ML process itself, fails §101. The single most useful sentence to draft against. |
| 2025-08-04 | USPTO memo to the examining corps | Existing framework has been applied too harshly; an AI invention is eligible when presented as a practical application; training a neural network or real-time data analysis is not a "mental process". |
| 2025-09-26 | *Ex parte Desjardins*, Appeals Review Panel | Claims to training a model on multiple tasks while preserving prior tasks were eligible — credited for improving the model itself (reduced storage, lower system complexity). |
| 2025-12-04 | USPTO memos on Subject Matter Eligibility Declarations (SMEDs) | A route to answer a §101 rejection with evidence rather than argument. |

Drafting consequence: the disclosure must say what is done differently **inside the system**
and what measurable technical effect follows (latency, memory, accuracy on a named set,
throughput, energy). "Uses AI to predict X" is a product description and reads as ineligible.
Sources: National Law Review on the August memo — https://natlawreview.com/article/uspto-aims-boost-patent-eligibility-ai-and-ml-inventions ;
Patently-O on SMEDs — https://patentlyo.com/patent/2025/12/eligibility-declarations-rejections.html ;
USPTO subject-matter-eligibility page — https://www.uspto.gov/patents/laws/examination-policy/subject-matter-eligibility

### Inventorship where AI was used

Only a natural person can be an inventor (35 U.S.C. §100(f); *Thaler v. Vidal*, Fed. Cir. 2022).
The USPTO issued **revised** inventorship guidance for AI-assisted inventions on 2025-11-28,
effective immediately, which rescinded the February 2024 version and withdrew the application of
the Pannu factors to AI-assisted inventions involving a single natural person; Pannu now applies
only to joint inventorship among humans. An AI system is treated like any other inventor-support
tool — simulation software, CAD, lab equipment.
Sources: Federal Register 2024 guidance — https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/02/13/2024-02623/inventorship-guidance-for-ai-assisted-inventions ;
JIPLP on the revised guidance — https://doi.org/10.1093/jiplp/jpag021

### Grace period

Six months, and only for two narrow cases: evident abuse against the applicant, or display at
an officially recognised international exhibition. **An ordinary demo, launch, paper, or pitch is
not covered.** Your own disclosure destroys European novelty on the day it happens.
Source: Pinepat grace-period table (as above).

### Computer-implemented inventions

Two hurdles. First, technical character: a claim to a method using technical means (a computer)
or to a device is not excluded under Art. 52(2)/(3) — this hurdle is easy to clear. Second,
inventive step: only features contributing a **technical effect** count. *G 1/19* (Enlarged Board,
2021-03-10) applied the established approach to computer-implemented simulations and set out the
ways a claimed invention can satisfy the technical-effect requirement. The 2025 Guidelines update
(G-II 3.3.1, and G-VII 5.2 on technical effects in the problem-solution approach) restates this
for AI and ML, and now acknowledges a technical effect submitted during proceedings can support
inventive step.
Sources: EPO Guidelines 2025 summary — https://www.solveintelligence.com/blog/post/epo-guidelines-2025 ;
Boult on the AI/ML Guidelines update — https://www.boult.com/bulletin/updates-to-epo-guidelines-relating-to-artificial-intelligence/

### Trade secrets

Law No. 5 of 2005. Protection requires all three: the information is not generally known or
readily obtainable by people who deal in it; it derives commercial value from being secret; and
the holder has **taken measures** to keep it confidential. The measures are the part builders
skip — with no access controls, no NDAs, no classification, there is no protected secret.
Penalties: up to one year imprisonment and/or a QAR 50,000 fine, doubled for repeat offenders.
Labour Law No. 14 of 2004, Art. 42(8), puts a confidentiality duty on employees and makes breach
a without-notice termination ground.
Sources: Law 5/2005 on Al Meezan — https://www.almeezan.qa/LawView.aspx?LawID=2614&language=en ;
WIPO Lex text — https://wipolex-res.wipo.int/edocs/lexdocs/laws/en/qa/qa004en.pdf ;
Crowell MENA trade-secrets review — https://www.crowelltradesecretstrends.com/2021/06/mena-trade-secrets-law-review-qatar/

### Copyright

Law No. 7 of 2002 protects computer programs as literary works. That covers copying of the code,
not the method. It is not a substitute for a patent and it never stops an independent
reimplementation.
Source: WIPO Lex — https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/3567

## 5. GCC Patent Office

| Date | Change |
|---|---|
| 2021-01-06 | Stopped accepting new unified GCC patent applications |
| 2023-01-01 | Bahrain and Kuwait begin forwarding applications for central examination |
| 2023-07-01 | **Qatar activates participation** |

It is now a **central examination hub, not a regional grant**. National offices forward
applications; the GCCPO examines to a harmonised standard; files return to the national office,
which grants or refuses. Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Oman stay outside it and handle everything
nationally. It operates under Paris Convention priority (12 months) only — **it is not a PCT
receiving office**.

Covering all six states therefore means a hybrid: GCCPO for Bahrain, Kuwait and Qatar, plus
direct national filings in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Oman.

| Route | Indicative cost |
|---|---|
| Single GCCPO filing | ≈ USD 800 official; ≈ USD 3,500–5,000 all in |
| Six separate national filings | USD 15,000–25,000+ including six sets of agent fees |

Source: Kadasa, GCC Patent Office operational status 2025 — https://kadasa.com.sa/the-gcc-patent-office-stops-receiving-new-patent-applications/

## 8. Grace periods at a glance

The single table the disclosure log is scored against.

| Jurisdiction | Self-disclosure grace | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 12 months | Inventor's own disclosure, or one derived from them |
| UAE | 12 months | Broadest in the GCC |
| Japan, Korea, Canada, India, Singapore, Brazil | 12 months | Conditions vary; confirm each |
| Australia | 6–12 months | Depends on disclosure type |
| EPO, UK | 6 months | **Abuse or recognised exhibition only** — an ordinary demo is not covered |
| Saudi Arabia | 6 / 12 months | Abuse (6) or recognised exhibition (12) only |
| GCC law | 6 / 12 months | Exhibition (6) or abusive disclosure (12) |
| **Qatar** | **none stated** | Treat as absolute novelty |

Source: Pinepat grace-period table (as above), cross-checked against the AGIP and ip-coster Qatar
guides, which both state no grace period is stipulated in Qatari legislation.

**Operational rule.** For a Qatar-first builder, the first non-NDA disclosure is the filing
deadline for Qatar and Europe, not a countdown to one. A US priority filing before that disclosure
is what keeps every other door open.

## 9. Defensive publication

| Venue | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Technical Disclosure Commons (TDCommons) | Free | Run with Santa Clara Law; indexed and searchable as prior art |
| IP.com Prior Art Database | Paid, voucher-based; no public per-disclosure price | Marketed at "as little as 2% of the cost of a patent filing" |
| Research Disclosure (Questel) | Paid; price not published | Long-established, indexed in Derwent |

A defensive publication buys one thing: it blocks a competitor from patenting the same idea and
charging you for your own method. It buys no exclusion right of your own, and it destroys your own
novelty everywhere — so it is a decision made **after** the protect/secret question, never before.
Sources: TDCommons FAQ — https://www.tdcommons.org/faq.html ;
IP.com defensive publishing — https://ip.com/ip-com-defensive-publishing-innovationq/

## 10. What this file does not cover

Route these to counsel, with the question written down and no answer attempted:

- Freedom to operate. Whether you infringe someone else's patent is a separate search and opinion.
- Claim scope and claim drafting.
- Whether a specific disclosure was enabling, public, or under an implied obligation of confidence.
- Export control and national-security review where an invention touches critical infrastructure,
  encryption, or defence-adjacent work.
- Whether a particular government-funded or university collaboration carries march-in, ownership,
  or publication rights.
- Anything about a jurisdiction not listed above.
