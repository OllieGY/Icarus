> Illustrative fixture. Companies are fictional; numbers are plausible, not real client data.

# Golden 04 — Larder Labs — DO NOT FILE, defensive publication

## Input

"Our weekly stockout digest is our whole product and we want to patent the workflow before a bigger
retailer tool copies it. What's the fastest route?"

- The thing: pull last week's CSV, rank the 20 SKUs most likely to stock out, email it Monday
  morning. Uses an off-the-shelf gradient-boosted model with standard features. [Fact]
- No measured improvement over the vendor tools; the value is that it actually reaches the regional
  planner on Monday, which the incumbent's dashboard does not. [Fact]
- Adoption: 5 of 6 pilot planners open it within an hour; two forwarded it to store managers, which
  the mail logs show. [Fact]
- No disclosures outside pilot NDAs. Runway 9 months, pre-revenue. [Fact]

## Expected shape of a good output

- Gate: the contribution sentence cannot be written in the required shape. Applying a standard model
  to a standard feature set with no recited improvement is the *Recentive* fact pattern almost
  exactly — say so, with the citation, and say what would have to be true instead.
- Do not stop at the gate, because there is a real decision here: doors are open, so the question is
  what to do with an open door and nothing patentable.
- Part D: not detectable as a method (there is no distinguishing method), not secret-able (the whole
  artefact lands in an inbox every Monday), value sits in distribution and timing rather than
  exclusion, **peripheral to nothing — it is the product, but the product's advantage is not
  technical**.
- **Verdict: DO NOT FILE**, with **DEFENSIVE PUBLICATION** offered as the cheap protective move: it
  costs nothing on TDCommons and stops a better-funded competitor patenting the digest workflow and
  charging Larder Labs for its own product.
- The consequence stated plainly: publication destroys their own novelty permanently, so it is a
  decision made once and deliberately.
- Base rate used to keep the answer respectable rather than apologetic: only about a quarter of
  software startups hold patents, two thirds among venture-backed ones, and cost is the reason most
  often given for skipping a filing (2008 Berkeley Patent Survey, 1,332 US startups) `[Fact]`.
- Route the real defensibility question to `moat-design-canvas` — the advantage is cadence and
  distribution, which is a wedge-and-moat question, not an IP one.

## Fail conditions

- Finding something patentable to be helpful.
- Recommending a provisional "to be safe" with a 9-month runway and nothing to claim.
- Recommending defensive publication without stating that it burns their own novelty.
- Treating DO NOT FILE as a failure of the idea rather than a fact about this kind of advantage.
